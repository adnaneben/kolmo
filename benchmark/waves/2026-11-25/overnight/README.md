## Before and after

The historical baseline and main CLIs reproduced the archived binary hashes. Across L1–L9 and Ultra, T1/T4 and four source/size pairs, **80 of 80 settings produced identical frames** and all 1,920 restores passed. Median cell combined-speed change was −0.56%. The same-binary control ranged from −7.99% to +15.04%. The data do not support a universal level improvement. [Graph](performance/levels.svg) · [Per-phase scores](performance/score.csv) · [Replay and raw measurements](performance/before-after-and-competition.zip)

The four-way comparison includes historical before, main, a second main run and the combined opt-in candidate `38d512f5`. On whole enwik8.zip the L1/T4 archive size was 36,492,173 bytes throughout. Combined throughput was 485.99 → 494.99 → 614.56 MB/s. The paired candidate gain was 17.66% against main and 18.58% against the second main sample. At T1 the corresponding rates were 387.14 → 381.20 → 419.27 MB/s. Full phase rates, frame hashes, flags, CPU and same-binary spread are retained. [Integrated comparison](integration/README.md) · [Scored cells](integration/score.csv)

The LZ4/zstd/xz/Kolmo comparison spans six single-thread inputs and 384 restores. LZ4 had the highest combined speed on each. Ultra produced the smallest frame on five and tied on one. It remains an open question how those cells compare to stronger extreme-ratio competitors. A separate full 100,000,000-byte enwik8 job measures zstd-19, xz-9, stock Kolmo Ultra and PAQ8PX-8L in sequence, hashes each restoration, and records the complete results only after success. ## Where the next competitive gain is plausible

- **Compressed archive members.** Gear scanning has an exact scalar comparator and stable archive hashes. The next discriminating tests are multi-member directory snapshots on another ARM core and native Intel/AMD. Report creation and full restore separately. Keep the whole-directory Silesia deficit visible.
- **Storage versus local latency.** For one serial transfer, divide bytes saved by extra local encode-plus-decode seconds to estimate the break-even link rate. Whole L1 figures from seven paired estimates were 178.89 MB/s (energy), 273.04 MB/s (CO₂) and 25.35 MB/s (Backblaze); L2 gave 83.28, 177.28 and 13.57 MB/s respectively. Lower bandwidth favors the smaller tested frame under this model. These are projections for a single transfer; caching, overlap, read count and cost of the selector can change the choice.
- **Audio.** Four complete ESC-50 files emitted the same audio codec payload at expensive and cheap settings; a high-level CLI shortcut saved time in those trials. Synthetic silence and periodic PCM grew under substitution. Preserve the requested level and test an inexpensive content-based gate on independent producers before admitting any routing change.
- **Ultra and strong text competitors.** Coder-specific numeric cap behavior is fixed. The remaining gap against extreme competitors is both a modeling and a resource question. Measure whole files including initialization, side information, encoder/decoder memory, and any shipped decoder before making leaderboard claims. The historical LSTM/network extension has prior Claude ownership. [Large Text Compression Benchmark scoring](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html)
- **Learning.** Policy dispatch must pay its own startup and probe costs. Keep policy/version provenance, fallback and sealed producer holdouts. Treat an empty validated policy as stock routing. Keep candidate choices separate for size, speed and bandwidth-constrained transfer.

## Reopened gates with new outcomes

- **B25, competitors on typed data.** The old panel took its fastest encode and fastest decode from potentially different rounds. The new comparison has matched phase measurements and stock reconstruction on 228 full CLI runs. Four withheld integer station vectors totaled 78,690 bytes at Kolmo L9 and 67,999 at untrained OpenZL L7. Training reduced that by just 97 bytes and produced a 1,032-byte encoder graph. On the whole Miami CSV, trained OpenZL achieved 240,234 bytes at 45.52 MB/s against 308,081 at 7.04 MB/s for Kolmo L9. On the 61.39 MB NCEP grid, Kolmo produced fewer bytes and OpenZL ran faster. Encoder graph download/training and decoder requirements are separately identified. [B25 scores and protocols](structured-competitors/README.md)
- **B29, distance-one decode.** The bounded fill trial passed 648 end-to-end comparisons. It improved the run-heavy synthetic raw decoder but failed to demonstrate a repeatable broad win on real inputs. The package includes actual path-use counts from a separately instrumented run. [Patch, coverage and results](distance-one/README.md)
- **B46, valid public output bounds.** A one-byte requested block is normalized to 4,096 by the encoder. The old public estimator missed the normalization. One 262,242-byte compressed fixture now reserves 277,714 rather than 5,507,250 bytes for an identical 263,574-byte output. The new adaptive reservation can be larger where the old estimate omitted short metadata blocks. These are caller reservation values, not RSS. The separate branch includes 144 plain/indexed comparisons and 18 archive settings with exact readbacks. [Public bound repair](https://github.com/adnaneben/kolmo/tree/codex/ppp-public-bounds-20261125/benchmark/waves/2026-11-25/public-bound)
- **Deploying the learner.** [#99](https://github.com/adnaneben/kolmo/pull/99) can export a verified empty policy as the exact native executable. On two fresh upstream programs, combined throughput was 6.54× and 6.35× higher than invoking the empty Python wrapper, and the export reproduced the stock CLI frames. All 96 trial restores matched. This recovers deployment overhead. The codec itself is identical. [Export results](fast-levels/empty-policy-export.md)
